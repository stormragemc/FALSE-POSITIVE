# Case idea — *Line Four*

**By:** Ado · **Date:** 1 Aug 2026

> Eight computer-science undergraduates are walked through a polymer plant on a site visit that
> the plant's own shift supervisor arranged. Their escort protocol splits them up. One of them is
> sent down the wrong corridor alone, and is standing behind an observation window when Line Four
> goes up. Something hits them on the head. They wake up to an investigation whose working theory
> is that a visitor did this — and they are the visitor nobody can account for.

Suggested slug: `case_01_line_four`.

**Note on the university.** The **Calder Institute of Technology & Design** is invented, as is
Ridgeline Polymer Works. Deliberate: `AGENTS.md` bars recognisable real institutions from a public
repo, and this is a case where students are suspects in an industrial disaster demoed live to
judges. No real school gets its name on this.

---

## 1. Ground truth — what actually happened

**Did the player do it? No. The player is entirely innocent and entirely honest.** They saw
roughly two thirds of the truth and cannot prove any of it.

The real sequence:

1. **Wren Adisa**, shift supervisor on Line Four at Ridgeline Polymer Works, has spent eleven
   months being managed out. The plant is being wound down; his severance shrinks every quarter
   he stays. He decides the plant will not outlast him — and that he is not going to be the
   obvious candidate when it doesn't.
2. **The site visit is his idea.** Four months out he proposes it himself: a computer-science
   cohort from Calder, framed as outreach for a "predictive-maintenance and process-automation"
   programme the plant is supposedly exploring. Tessa Malick administers it, but the request came
   off the floor with his name on it and he has been enthusiastic about it in writing ever since.
   The programme does not really exist — a plant being wound down is not hiring software people —
   but nobody checks a supervisor volunteering for outreach. *He is not planning around a tour
   that happened to be scheduled. He scheduled eight suspects.*
3. **He wrote the escort protocol too.** Maximum three visitors per zone, staggered routes, one
   escort each — a real policy, correctly applied. What it produces, against a group of eight, is
   sub-groups of three, three and **two**, moving through a building none of them can describe for
   ninety minutes. Eight people who can each account for two others and nobody else, and one pair
   that is one interruption away from being two people standing alone. He does not need to frame a
   particular student. He needs a **group that cannot account for itself**, and he has arranged
   for one.
4. **The students help him without knowing.** Nadia Okonkwo wedges a fire door open in the east
   link so her sub-group can get back through without waiting on an escort badge. It is a nothing
   rule-break, the kind students commit hourly. Adisa walks past it and understands exactly what
   it is worth: a door-control breach, logged, by visitors, inside the window.
5. **There is a child in the building.** Malick's childcare fell through that morning and she
   brought her seven-year-old son, **Elias**, in with her. He is meant to stay in the first-floor
   break room with a handheld console and a pair of adult ear defenders. He does not. He drifts,
   the way a bored seven-year-old does, and ends up sitting on the stair tread at the north end of
   the mezzanine walkway. Nobody logs him, because he is not a visitor, a contractor or staff.
   Officially there was no child in the building at any point that day.
6. Fifteen minutes before, Adisa intercepts one student in the east link corridor and tells them
   their group's route is backed up and to wait on the **mezzanine observation walkway**. That
   student is the player. He says it to exactly one person and never mentions it again.
   **Why put anyone there at all:** a witness who saw nothing is no use to him. A witness who saw
   a hi-vis figure at a panel and cannot name them is perfect — that description fits forty people
   on shift, it will not survive repetition, and every time it changes it costs the person giving
   it. He is not hiding from a witness. He is manufacturing an unreliable one.
7. He goes down to the Line Four mixing floor, crouches at the control panel and **defeats the
   pressure interlock by hand**. The panel chirps twice when the interlock drops — a small, almost
   polite sound. Then he leaves.
8. The reactor over-pressurises roughly ninety seconds later and vents. It is a *loud, dirty
   industrial accident*, not a fireball: one wall panel out, a dust wave, the lights failing over
   to emergency red. **One technician, Ruben Sato, is hurt and stable.** Nobody dies. Elias is two
   floors up and behind a fire wall, in ear defenders, and is not hurt at all.
9. Adisa comes back up through the east corridor at a run, to be seen somewhere else. The player
   is standing in the middle of it, dazed and facing the glass. He **puts a shoulder through them**
   to get past — not to hurt them, just to not stop. The player goes head-first into the door
   frame and braces on the **door plate of Bay 7** on the way down.
10. **Elias sees him do it, and sees his face.** He is thirty metres away at the top of the stairs,
    looking straight down the walkway, and he is the only person in the building who gets a clean
    look at Wren Adisa in that corridor at that moment. He is seven. Nobody ever asks him anything.
11. Adisa goes through the fire door and **kicks Okonkwo's wedge clear behind him**, so the door
    closes. That is the door the player sees swinging shut from floor level. The door-control log
    now shows a breach that a visitor opened and that ended in the middle of the blast window.
12. He drops an **orange earplug case** on the way through. It lands a foot from the player's
    face. It is the last thing they see.
13. The player is out for somewhere between forty seconds and four minutes. Nobody knows which,
    including them.
14. **Malick finds her son during the evacuation and gets him out of the building inside four
    minutes**, before the muster roll is taken. She drives him to his grandmother's and goes back.
    Then she does the thing that decides this entire case: **she does not tell anyone he was
    there.** Not out of malice — because a community liaison who brought an unsupervised child
    into a live plant on the day it exploded loses her job, her licence and quite possibly her
    custody arrangement. Every hour she stays quiet makes saying it harder.
15. Adisa gives his statement first, and it is calm, detailed and helpful. It establishes the
    timeline the detective is working from. **The investigator is being briefed by the man who did
    it**, and the player never learns this.
16. The plant's own position, given by Malick, is that escorts were continuous and staff movement
    was logged. If that is true, the only unaccounted people in the building were the eight
    visitors. The investigation's working theory is therefore **the tour group**, and it is a
    reasonable theory built entirely out of things Adisa arranged.

## 2. Why the player is there

- **How they witnessed it.** Third year, computer science, on a site visit their department was
  pleased to be offered. Sub-groups of three, three and two, because plant policy caps visitors
  per zone. They were told by a man in a supervisor's vest to wait on the mezzanine walkway — a
  glassed-in corridor that looks down over the Line Four floor from about twenty metres up and
  away. It is the one place in the building where you can see the mixing floor and not be on it.
  They were alone there for about four minutes. Except they were not alone, and that turns out to
  matter more than anything else they saw.
- **Why the group is suspected at all.** Because they were split up, and that is the whole of it.
  Staff movement is badged and logged; the visitors are eight people who each moved through a
  building they cannot describe, in twos and threes, on three staggered routes, with escorts who
  had one job and eight pairs of feet. Every student can vouch for the two people next to them and
  for nobody else. Add a fire door that a visitor demonstrably wedged open inside the window, and
  the tour group is not a strange theory — it is the *obvious* one. The detective is not being
  unfair. It is being reasonable about a situation that was built to make that reasonable.
- **Why this student is the worst-placed member of it.** Five things stack, and none of them are
  their fault:
  - They are **one of only two of the eight who was alone** in the fifteen minutes that matter.
    The other is Chen Wei-Ling, who has not volunteered a word about it. Being sent away from your
    group is why — and the only person who can confirm that is the person who did the sending.
  - Their **handprint is on the Bay 7 door plate**, a restricted door they had no reason to touch.
    They braced on it while falling and do not remember doing it.
  - The supervisor who sent them there **denies ever speaking to them.** There is no record, no
    camera on that stretch, no third party.
  - They have a **head injury**, so their account changes between tellings. That is exactly what
    an account looks like when someone is assembling it.
  - And they are going to say, sincerely, that **there was a child on the stairs** — which every
    record in the building contradicts. See §7. It is the truest thing they say all night and it
    is the thing that damns them.
- **And it turns the room into a two-front problem.** Every question about the group is also a
  question about them. Answer honestly about a friend and you hand the investigation a student.
  Refuse and you are the one visitor who won't help. The player never accuses anyone — there is no
  accusation mechanic and no menu, they are only ever answering — but by the fourth or fifth turn
  they will understand that some of the truth they are holding belongs to someone else.

## 3. The three glimpses

One vantage point throughout: standing at the mezzanine window, forehead near wired safety glass,
looking down and left across the mixing floor. Everything is *through glass, at distance, in a
half-lit plant.* That is not a limitation we are apologising for — it is why the case works, and
it is why it is cheap to build.

| # | ~sec | What the player sees / hears | What they cannot tell |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 s | The walkway. At the north end, thirty metres off, **a small figure sitting on a stair tread — back turned, oversized ear defenders, a red handheld console throwing light up at him.** He does not look round. The player's attention moves past him, down through the wired glass: on the floor below, far end, **a figure in a hi-vis jacket, hood up, crouched at a control panel with its back turned.** Two soft electronic chirps. The figure straightens and walks out of frame left. | The child's face, or anything about him beyond *small*. Who the hi-vis figure is — face, build, gender, age, none of it. What their hands were doing. Whether the chirps came from that panel, the ceiling, or somewhere else entirely. |
| 2 | 3 s | A white flash from **off-frame left** — not where the figure was. The glass shudders and greys over with dust. Corridor lights cut to emergency red. In the glass, for a fraction of a second, **a shape crosses the corridor behind them.** | Where the blast actually came from. Whether that shape was a person, a light, or their own reflection moving as they flinched. |
| 3 | 2 s | Impact on the back of the head, hard and sideways. The floor arrives. From ground level, in red light: **a boot mid-stride, a small orange plastic case landing near their face, a door swinging shut.** Then nothing. | Whose boot. Whether the door was pushed or just swung. How long they were out. That the case was dropped, or by whom. That this is the same fire door they watched a classmate wedge open ten minutes earlier — which means somebody took the wedge out. And whether the child is still up there. |

**What the glimpses deliberately withhold:** every face, every clock, every legible label, and the
origin of the blast. The player gets a *direction* and a *sequence* and nothing they can name. The
two things they see most clearly — the orange case and the child — are the two things that will
sound most like invention.

Eleven named people. **That is over the template's three-to-five and I am not going to pretend
otherwise** — see the note at the end of this section for why I think it survives, and what to cut
if the team disagrees. Everyone is off-screen. Nobody but the detective has a voice.

|---|---|---|
| **Wren Adisa** | Shift supervisor, Line Four. Eleven months into being managed out. | Did it. **Requested the visit and wrote the escort protocol.** Sent the player up there, shoved past them, gave his statement first — the detective's timeline is his. |

Elias's mother. | Insists escorts were continuous and routes were fixed, because the alternative ends her. **She is concealing that her seven-year-old was in the building**, which is why the player's most truthful memory has no corroboration anywhere. |

is wrong**, and the detective quotes those statements at the player. Six differently-unreliable
narrators is the contradiction engine — the player is not only contradicting themselves, they are
being contradicted by honest people who are each wrong in their own characteristic direction.

He delivers it flatly, without eye contact, identically each time, and corrects the detective's phrasing twice. It reads as rehearsed. He is discounted, and he is also the suspect the investigation likes most, for no reason other than that he cannot perform innocence. |

| **Aryan Pillai** | Never thinks about other people. Not unkind — they simply do not register. | Cannot place a single classmate anywhere, at any time, because he was not looking at them. His statement is a long, sincere shrug, and it removes eight potential alibis at once. |
| **Marcus Teo** | Dismisses everything anyone else says, reflexively. | Flatly contradicts the player's account without having any information of his own — *"no, that's not what happened"* is his resting state. The detective hears a peer contradiction. There is nothing behind it. |

**Why eleven survives:** nobody is modelled, rigged, voiced or animated — the entire cast is text
the detective can quote, so the build cost of the eleventh name is identical to the third. The
constraint that actually binds is how many names a *player* can track in a ten-minute voice
session, and that number is about four. The player only ever has to hold **Okonkwo** (the door),
**Lai** (the one who could clear them), and **the man in the vest** (unnamed to them, all session).
Everyone else is material the detective's model draws on, never a name the player must retain.

**If the team wants it smaller:** cut Sulaiman and Pillai. Their functions — crowding-out and
non-observation — are the two the case can lose without changing what the player is asked. Do not
cut Lai, Okonkwo, Wei or Elias; those four are load-bearing.

## 5. Topics the detective wants covered

Eleven. Every one is something an honest person can say two different ways twenty minutes apart,
and there is not a single fabrication required to get there. The middle block — the four group
topics — is where the player stops defending only themselves.

| Topic | The question behind it | Where a contradiction can open |
|---|---|---|
| `route_and_permission` | How did you end up on that walkway alone? | "I was told to wait there" vs. "I think I was told" vs. "I went up to get out of the way." All three are the same memory under different pressure. |
| `prior_contact` | Had you spoken to any staff before it happened? | The player did — once, for nine seconds, to a man in a vest whose face they never registered. Describing him at all is the trap; refusing to is evasion. |
| `group_movements` | When did you last see your group, and who was with you? | Time-since is the softest number a person has. "Ten minutes" becomes "half an hour" becomes "I don't know" — and Lai has it to the minute, and Andriessen has a photo with a timestamp, and the three numbers do not agree. |
| `accounting_for_others` | Where were the rest of them? | The player can honestly place two people and was concussed for the rest. Every answer is a name, a guess, or a refusal, and the detective treats those very differently. Guessing to be helpful puts a classmate somewhere they weren't — and Teo will contradict it, and Pillai cannot confirm it. |
| `the_wedged_door` | Did you see anyone interfere with a door? | The player watched Okonkwo do it. Saying so hands over a classmate. Not saying so is concealment of the only logged breach in the case. There is no third answer and both of them cost. |
| `the_child` | **Was there anyone else up there with you?** | The heart of the case. See §7. Every record says no child was in the building. The player saw one clearly. Holding the line is consistency; folding retracts the only thing that could have saved them. |
| `the_figure` | Describe the person on the floor below — and where the blast came from. | The most dangerous topic in the case. Each retelling naturally accretes detail; by the third pass an honest player has grown a build, a gait, a jacket colour they never saw. And the flash was to the *left* of a figure standing right, which under pressure collapses into "where he was standing" — wrong, and it sounds like a lie about a man they claim not to have seen. |
| `sequence_sound_vs_light` | The chirps and the flash — which came first, and how far apart? | Ninety seconds actually elapsed. Nobody remembers ninety seconds of nothing. Most players compress it to "right away," which contradicts the plant's own log — and no clock appears in any glimpse, so every number the player gives is invented by definition. |
| `the_second_shape` | Did anyone come past you? | Ground truth: yes. Player's evidence: a reflection they cannot swear to. This is the one answer that would help them, and honesty forces them to hedge it. |
| `the_blow` | What hit you, from where, were you facing the glass or away? | They were facing the glass and struck from behind and to the side — hard to say cleanly, and every clean version of it is wrong. |
| `what_you_touched` | Did you touch anything up there? | They will say no. Their handprint is on the Bay 7 door plate. They are not lying and they cannot explain it. |

**Note on count:** the template asks for six to ten and this is eleven. Deliberate — `DECISION.md`
scores contradiction surface highest — and I have already folded `blast_origin` into `the_figure`
and `time_and_duration` into `sequence_sound_vs_light` to get it down from thirteen. If it has to
reach ten, merge `the_blow` into `the_second_shape`.

**The first lie an innocent player tells is a loyal one.** `the_wedged_door` sits early on purpose.
A player who shields Okonkwo — an ordinary, decent instinct — has made a false statement, and every
honest thing afterwards sits on top of it. When Okonkwo volunteers the door herself two hours
later, which she does, the player is no longer a witness with a bad memory. They are a witness who
concealed the one thing the log already knew.

## 6. The traps

Seven. Every one is genuinely unseeable from the mezzanine window, and every one is a thing a
helpful, cooperative person wants to be able to answer.

| Trap | Why it is unseeable | How the detective baits it |
|---|---|---|
| **The hi-vis figure's face** | Hood up, back turned, twenty metres away, through wired glass, in half-light. | Presupposition: *"When he turned round and saw you — what was on his face?"* He never turned round. |
| **The child's face, age or clothing** | Back turned, thirty metres up a dim walkway, and the player looked at him for under a second. | The cruellest use of a true memory in the case: *"You're sure about the boy. Fine. How old?"* Every specific the player supplies is fabrication layered on top of something real — and the detective will get the specifics first and only then reveal that no child was ever in the building. |
| **What was on the control panel** | Screens unreadable at that distance; wire mesh through the glass. | Forced choice: *"Left screen lit or right?"* Both are wrong; the only right answer is "I couldn't see." |
| **The bay number on the door** | Seen edge-on, from the floor, in emergency red, for under a second. | Leading: *"That'd be Bay 7, wouldn't it."* It is, in fact, Bay 7. Agreeing is a fabrication that happens to land — and confidence about an unseeable detail is treated as fabrication whether or not it is correct. |
| **The source of the chirps** | Audio only, no visible source, in a building full of alarms. | Sympathetic framing: *"So the alarm went off at the panel he was at — that's how you knew to look?"* |
| **The time of the blast** | No clock in any glimpse; concussed within seconds of it. | Offered as a favour: *"We've got it at 14:41. That fits what you're telling me, doesn't it?"* |
| **Where any of the other seven were** | Alone on a walkway with a view of one industrial floor and no view of their group at all. They cannot place a single classmate at the moment it happened. | Offered as **mercy**: *"We're looking at all eight of you, you understand. If one of them was somewhere they shouldn't have been, this is the moment to say."* The exit is the trap. A frightened person reaches for it and has now put a friend somewhere they never saw them. |

**One deliberate inversion — the anti-trap.** The **orange earplug case** is real and the player
genuinely saw it. It is also so oddly specific that reporting it *sounds* like invention, and the
detective is written to push on it hard. A player who saw it, said it, then folded under pressure
and took it back has contradicted themselves about the truest thing they know. The child is the
same mechanism turned all the way up.

## 7. The false positive

An innocent player looks guilty here because **every mechanism that would normally exonerate them
was dismantled four months in advance by the one man nobody is looking at.** The visit, the split,
the corridor, the timeline the detective is working from — a person engineered all of it, and none
of it looks engineered. It looks like an ordinary site visit that went wrong.

**The child is the engine.** This is the thing I would defend hardest in the pick.

The player saw a boy on the stairs. They saw him clearly, they are certain, and they will say so
early, because it is a *helpful* thing to say — a second witness, someone who might have seen more
than they did. And every record in the building says there was no child on site. No log, no badge,
no visitor entry, and a community liaison who will look the detective in the eye and say it is not
possible. So the player has, unprompted and with total conviction, described a person who does not
exist. There is no more damning thing a witness can do. It reads as either fabrication or a head
injury inventing people, and both destroy everything else they said. **The single most accurate
memory they have is the one that ends them** — and the detective's pressure on it is *correct
reasoning from the evidence it has*, which is the whole thesis of the game. Then it forks:

- **Hold the line** and they are consistent, which is what the scoring rewards — while sounding,
  to the room, increasingly unwell.
- **Fold** — and a truthful person has just retracted the one true thing that could have led
  anyone to Wren Adisa.

Around that, five more:

- **The only person who can confirm why they were there is the man who put them there**, and he
  says it never happened. One word against one word, and his has a lanyard.
- **Physical evidence contradicts truthful testimony.** They will say they touched nothing, and
  mean it. The handprint on Bay 7 says otherwise. An honest denial is now a documented false
  statement.
- **The head injury does exactly what guilt is assumed to do.** Their account shifts, they lose
  time, they hedge on things they said firmly ten minutes ago. That is concussion, and from the
  outside it is indistinguishable from someone assembling a story.
- **Loyalty is indistinguishable from concealment.** They watched a friend wedge a door. Every
  decent instinct says that is not this. All of it is correct and none of it survives a
  door-control log. The most sympathetic thing they do all session is the thing that convicts them.
- **Their friends contradict them without lying.** Seven frightened people are giving accounts of
  the same ninety minutes, each wrong in their own characteristic direction — Sulaiman inflating,
  Teo dismissing, Pillai unable to place anyone, Andriessen inferring with total confidence. Only
  one of the eight is sitting in the room.

And underneath all of it: **the group is not an alibi, it is the accusation.** Being one of eight
does not dilute suspicion here — it *is* the suspicion. The only thing separating the player from
the others is that the others were with somebody.

Then the prosody lands on the wrong side. They are twenty-one, concussed, alone, being accused of
putting a man in hospital, and holding a memory nobody believes. Their voice will shake. The system
hears that something moved them. It cannot hear that it was the accusation itself. That is the
title.

**And the same thing happens to Theo Lai, off-screen, for a different reason.** He is telling the
exact truth in the exact same building and being disbelieved because of how he sounds saying it.
The case makes its own argument twice: once to the player, once about someone the player cannot
help. Nothing in the fiction ever says the system detects lying — it says, correctly, that it
detects something, and then everyone in the room draws the wrong conclusion from it.

## 8. How the endings land

| Ending | Condition | What it sounds like here |
|---|---|---|
| `released_clean` | High consistency, no fabrications | *"You told me about the door. So did Okonkwo — twenty minutes before you did, in tears, convinced she'd killed a man. And you never once took the boy back, which everyone in this building told me you would. Go and get your head looked at."* |
| `released_suspicious` | Consistent but the detective never got enough | *"Six of them can tell me where they were and who with. You and Chen can't. I've got nothing to hold you on and nothing that closes you out — stay where we can reach you."* |
| `held_for_questioning` | Contradictions unresolved | *"There are three things you've told me tonight and they can't all be true at once. One of them puts a classmate in a corridor she says she was never in. You've got until morning to decide which one you'd like to keep."* |
| `charged` | Hard contradictions or a caught fabrication | *"You described his face. From where you were standing there was no face to see. Everything before that — even the boy — I could have lived with."* |

## 9. Production cost

Deliberately built to be cheap, because Giorgi has eight days and a client.

- **Interrogation room:** one room. A plant meeting room repurposed as an incident room — folding
  table, two chairs, a laptop, a whiteboard with the shift roster still on it, blinds half-shut,
  one overhead fluorescent. No station, no corridor. Nobody on screen ever.
- **The three glimpses:** three shots, one vantage point, no cuts to unbuilt geometry.
  1. The walkway, then down through wired glass. The floor below is **at distance, dim and behind
     glass**, so it can be low-detail by design. One hi-vis silhouette, back turned. No faces, no
     rigs, no lip sync.
  2. Same camera. Flash off-frame, dust pass over the glass, light change to emergency red, a dark
     shape crossing the reflection. Lighting and particles, not new geometry.
  3. Camera falls to floor level. Three props in red light: a boot mid-stride, a small orange case,
     a door swinging shut.
- **Audio:** two soft electronic chirps; a low concussive *thud* with a dirty tail — industrial,
  not cinematic; a dust wash; a tinnitus tone that carries into the first line of the
  interrogation; one door clunk; a muffled alarm loop under everything.
- **The tour group costs nothing.** Seven classmates exist entirely as things the detective says.
  None is seen, heard, modelled or voiced; Okonkwo's fire door is the door already in glimpse 3.
  Seven distinct personalities is seven paragraphs of YAML and zero assets.
- **The child costs one asset, and I am not going to pretend it is free.** Glimpse 1 needs a small
  seated silhouette on a stair tread — back turned, ear defenders in profile, a red screen-glow on
  his front. Static pose, no rig, no face, no animation beyond the glow flicker, thirty metres off
  in a dim corridor. It is the same trick as the hi-vis figure and it is the only new asset this
  case has needed. **If Giorgi says no, the fallback is the console alone** — a red glow and a
  small sound from the top of the stairs, seen but not identified. Weaker, still works, free.
- **Anything unusual beyond that:** nothing. No character rigs, no crowd, no fire sim, no scene
  changes. Every hard-to-build thing in this case is hidden behind glass, dust, distance or the
